# IcyAlert from zero: what we are actually doing

Updated: 2026-08-04

## 1. The real-world question

The broad question is:

> What controls the amount of Arctic sea ice left in summer, and can we predict unusually low or ice-free summers early enough?

The main outcome is **summer Arctic sea ice**. The first candidate causes/drivers are not soil. They are:

- how much/thick sea ice remains after winter;
- Arctic air temperature;
- heat transported into the Arctic by the ocean;
- heat arriving at the surface through shortwave and longwave radiation.

The related paper's simplest causal question is:

> Do March winter sea-ice volume, Arctic air temperature, and ocean heat transport influence September sea-ice extent—and by how much?

## 2. What is sea-ice extent, concentration, and volume?

These terms are different.

### Sea-ice concentration

For each ocean grid cell, sea-ice concentration is the fraction covered by ice.

- 0% means open water.
- 100% means completely ice-covered.

This is a spatial map.

### Sea-ice extent

Sea-ice extent counts the total area of ocean cells that are at least 15% ice-covered. A cell passing the threshold contributes its full area.

This produces one Arctic-wide number, usually in million square kilometres.

### Sea-ice area

Sea-ice area weights each cell by its actual ice fraction. A cell with 40% concentration contributes 40% of its area.

IcyAlert's public ice-free definition uses sea-ice **area** below 1 million square kilometres, while the Docquier causal benchmark studies September sea-ice **extent**. This target difference must be kept explicit.

### Sea-ice volume

Volume includes thickness as well as area. Thin winter ice can melt more easily than thick winter ice, so March volume is a possible precondition for September extent.

## 3. What is an observation?

An observation is information about the actual Earth. It is not always a perfect direct measurement.

### Satellite product

A satellite measures radiation reaching its sensor. A retrieval algorithm converts that signal into a geophysical variable such as sea-ice concentration. The result has noise, missing regions, resolution limits, and algorithmic uncertainty.

Example used by the related paper: OSI SAF sea-ice concentration/extent.

### In-situ observation

Measurements taken locally by buoys, ships, weather stations, aircraft, or ocean instruments. They can be accurate locally but sparse across the Arctic.

### Reanalysis

A reanalysis combines many real observations with a physical forecast model through data assimilation. It provides a complete gridded estimate even where observations are missing.

It is best understood as a physically consistent reconstruction—not a perfect observation.

Examples relevant here:

- ORAS5 for the ocean;
- PIOMAS for sea-ice volume;
- atmospheric reanalyses for air temperature and other fields.

### Synthetic observation

For a controlled experiment, take a hidden simulated truth, observe only selected parts, and add realistic noise:

$$
y_t = H(x_t) + \varepsilon_t.
$$

Here, $x_t$ is the complete hidden state, $H$ selects or transforms what the observing system can see, and $\varepsilon_t$ is observation error.

This lets us test a filter because the complete truth remains known.

## 4. What is a dataset?

A dataset is simply an organized collection of variables over space, time, and sometimes ensemble member.

A climate dataset may look conceptually like:

```text
time:       January 1970, February 1970, ...
latitude:   Arctic grid rows
longitude:  Arctic grid columns
member:     simulation 1, simulation 2, ...
variables:  ice concentration, ice thickness, air temperature, ocean temperature, velocity, ...
```

Two important dataset types are:

- **model data:** values generated by a simulator such as EC-Earth3;
- **observational/reanalysis data:** estimates of the actual Earth.

## 5. What is a physical process model?

A process model uses equations representing atmosphere, ocean, sea ice, land, and energy exchanges to move the climate state forward in time.

Conceptually:

$$
x_{t+1} = M(x_t, u_t) + \eta_t,
$$

where $x_t$ is the current climate state, $M$ is the process model, $u_t$ represents external inputs/forcing, and $\eta_t$ represents unresolved/model error.

EC-Earth3 is one such coupled physical process model. Its relevant components are:

- IFS/HTESSEL: atmosphere and land-surface physics;
- NEMO 3.6: ocean;
- LIM3: sea ice.

Running the full model requires a large scientific software and HPC workflow. CMIP6 gives us files already generated by such runs.

## 6. What is CMIP6?

CMIP6 is not one model and not one dataset. It is an international framework in which many climate-modelling groups run standardized experiments and publish their outputs in a common format.

Examples of participating models include EC-Earth3, CESM2, CanESM5, ACCESS-ESM1.5, and MPI-ESM1.2-LR.

Important CMIP6 experiments here:

- `historical`: simulations using historical external forcing;
- `ssp370`: a future scenario after 2014.

CMIP6 is valuable because it contains long, physically generated climate trajectories. However, a model's simulated year 2005 is not the exact weather sequence experienced by the real Earth in 2005.

## 7. What is an ensemble?

Climate is partly chaotic. Two runs started from almost identical states can gradually develop different weather sequences.

An ensemble is a collection of multiple runs. Instead of producing one future, it produces a distribution of possible futures.

The ensemble spread provides information about uncertainty and covariance—the central quantities needed by EnKF.

## 8. What is a SMILE?

SMILE means **Single-Model Initial-condition Large Ensemble**.

Break the phrase apart:

- **Single model:** every member uses the same process model, such as EC-Earth3.
- **Initial condition:** members begin with tiny differences in their starting states.
- **Large ensemble:** many members are run, for example 50.

They share the same external forcing, such as greenhouse-gas scenario, but their internal weather evolves differently.

Why this helps:

- separates forced climate change from chaotic internal variability;
- supplies many trajectories for training an emulator;
- provides ensemble uncertainty/covariance;
- allows some members to train the model and another member to act as hidden truth.

## 9. What is C3S seasonal data?

C3S combines seasonal forecast systems from multiple forecasting centres. These forecasts are initialized from estimates of the actual Earth and are associated with real forecast dates.

This makes C3S useful for asking:

> Starting from the estimated real winter state, can we improve the prediction for the following months or summer?

C3S is better aligned with real observations than free-running CMIP6 trajectories. But archived forecast files may not allow us to insert our filtered state back into the original forecasting model and rerun it. That requires executable/restart access or our own transition emulator.

## 10. What is an emulator?

An emulator is a smaller learned model that imitates part of the process model.

We train it using transitions from EC-Earth3 data:

$$
\widehat{x}_{t+1} = F_\theta(x_t).
$$

After training, inference means giving the emulator a current state and asking it to predict the next state.

If $F_\theta$ is differentiable, it can be used in gradient-based 4D-Var. It can also be used in EnKF, which does not require derivatives.

## 11. What does EnKF do?

EnKF alternates between prediction and correction.

1. The process model advances an ensemble of possible states.
2. A satellite/reanalysis observation arrives.
3. The filter compares predicted observations with the real observation.
4. It corrects each ensemble member according to uncertainty and covariance.
5. The corrected ensemble is propagated again.

A simple intuition:

```text
model forecast:      sea ice is probably around 5.0, but uncertain
satellite estimate:  sea ice appears closer to 4.6
filter analysis:     move the estimate toward 4.6, but not blindly
```

Covariance allows an observation of one variable to update another related variable. For example, observed ice concentration may update estimated ice thickness if the forecast ensemble shows a reliable relationship between them.

## 12. What does 4D-Var do?

4D-Var searches for an initial state that produces a model trajectory fitting observations throughout a time window while remaining reasonably close to the prior state.

It needs gradients through the forecast trajectory. Classical systems use tangent-linear and adjoint models. A differentiable neural emulator can provide gradients using automatic differentiation.

EnKF represents uncertainty with an ensemble. 4D-Var solves an optimization problem over a time window. We can compare them using the same emulator, truth, observations, and metrics.

## 13. Where does LK causality enter?

LKIF asks whether changes in one time series carry directed information into changes in another.

The first causal target is September summer sea-ice extent. Candidate drivers include:

- March winter sea-ice volume;
- annual Arctic 2 m air temperature;
- Atlantic ocean heat transport;
- Pacific ocean heat transport;
- optionally surface radiation variables.

This is not proof from a controlled physical experiment. It is a directional causal estimate under mathematical assumptions and must be checked with physical understanding and alternative methods.

The later research idea is to investigate whether the evolving covariance from the online filter can support a time-varying LK calculation. That mathematical connection is not yet an established IcyAlert specification and must be derived and tested carefully.

## 14. The two experiments we now understand

### Experiment A: controlled synthetic filter test

```text
EC-Earth3 SMILE data
        |
train differentiable transition emulator
        |
hold out one member as hidden truth
        |
generate noisy/sparse synthetic observations
        |
compare EnKF, improved filter, and 4D-Var
        |
later inspect covariance for time-varying LK
```

This is the cleanest place to develop the filtering algorithm.

### Experiment B: real seasonal test

```text
C3S initialized seasonal ensemble
        + real satellite/reanalysis observations
        |
filter or correct seasonal forecast
        |
verify against the observed Earth
```

This tests whether the method helps on real forecast dates.

## 15. What we should decide before downloading data

We should first choose a small state and observation problem.

### Beginner-friendly first state

Use a small Arctic vector rather than a complete grid:

$$
x_t =
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{sea-ice extent} \\
\text{sea-ice volume} \\
\text{Arctic air temperature}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Possible first observation: noisy sea-ice extent alone.

This makes every forecast, covariance, Kalman update, and failure visible. After it works, add regional/grid variables and ocean heat transport.

### Decisions to freeze

- monthly or seasonal time step;
- state variables;
- observed variables;
- observation noise and missingness;
- training members and held-out truth members;
- forecast horizon;
- EnKF and 4D-Var evaluation metrics.

Only then should we download the exact EC-Earth3 fields required by that contract.

## 16. The immediate goal in one sentence

> Build a small EC-Earth3-based world in which a learned process model forecasts Arctic sea ice, incomplete/noisy observations correct it through an online filter, and the fully known held-out simulation tells us whether the filter improved the estimate.

LK covariance integration comes after this works.

## 17. Terminology checkpoint

The most important correction is:

> **SMILE is not a process model. SMILE is a way of running one process model many times.**

Use this mapping:

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| EC-Earth3 | A physical process model—the engine |
| One member | One run of that engine |
| Ensemble | A collection of runs |
| SMILE | Same engine run many times from slightly different starts |
| CMIP6 | Organized library of simulations from many climate models |
| C3S | Real-date seasonal forecasts from several forecasting systems |
| Satellite | Measurement of the actual Earth |
| Reanalysis | Observation-informed reconstruction of the actual Earth |

C3S is also model output; it is not the observation itself. A simple filtering experiment uses:

```text
C3S ensemble forecast = prior prediction
satellite/reanalysis  = observation/reference
filter                = combines and corrects them
```

CMIP6/SMILE becomes necessary when we want large simulated training data, a controlled hidden-truth experiment, or our own process emulator.

## 18. Where the filtering task belongs

Within IcyAlert, the filtering contribution fits most naturally in WP3:

```text
WP1 supplies data
        ↓
WP3 ensemble initialization + dynamic prediction
        ↓
WP4 ice-free-Arctic prediction
```

The later covariance-to-LKIF work connects WP3 back to WP2's causal-method work.
