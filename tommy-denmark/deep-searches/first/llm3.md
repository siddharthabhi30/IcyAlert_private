Integration of Liang-Kleeman Information Flow with Kalman Filtering and Data Assimilation: A Comprehensive AnalysisThe integration of Liang-Kleeman Information Flow (LKIF) with sequential data assimilation (DA) methods, specifically Kalman filtering (KF) and ensemble Kalman filters (EnKF), represents a novel and rapidly evolving frontier in tracing transient causal structures within nonstationary dynamical systems. Originally, LKIF was derived ab initio from the first principles of information theory and dynamical systems to quantify the directional transfer of information—and thus, causality—between variables. The foundational LKIF framework assumed systemic stationarity, relying on static sample covariances calculated over long time series to populate its estimators.However, environmental and geophysical systems, such as land-atmosphere interactions, are inherently nonstationary. Their statistical properties shift due to seasonal cycles, anomalous events, and changing boundary layer conditions, rendering stationary causal assumptions inadequate. Recent advancements have addressed this limitation by bifurcating the application of LKIF within filtering theory into two distinct methodological paradigms. The first paradigm utilizes the Kalman filter as an optimal state estimator to recursively compute time-varying covariance matrices, which are subsequently substituted into the LKIF equations to extract transient causality (the TvLK and MtvLK frameworks). The second paradigm reverses this relationship, utilizing LKIF as an independent metric to optimize the Kalman filter itself; specifically, by replacing empirical, distance-based covariance localization in ensemble filters with causality-driven localization to preserve physical interaction structures.This report exhaustively analyzes the theoretical mechanics, mathematical justifications, experimental validations, code implementations, and fundamental ambiguities characterizing the merger of LKIF with Kalman estimation error covariance, observation noise, and ensemble data assimilation.A. Directly Relevant PapersThe foundational texts executing the integration of LKIF with Kalman filtering approaches are analyzed in profound detail below, focusing on their mathematical adaptations, definitions of covariance, and handling of system noise.1. Hagan et al. (2019) – Time-Varying LK (TvLK)The paper by Hagan et al. (2019), titled "A Time-Varying Causality Formalism Based on the Liang–Kleeman Information Flow for Analyzing Directed Interactions in Nonstationary Climate Systems," serves as the genesis for filtering-based dynamic LKIF. The authors recognized that while LK causality provides an efficient tool for studying phenomena like El Niño and the Indian Ocean Dipole, its reliance on stationarity precludes the analysis of seasonally changing boundary layer properties in land-atmosphere interactions.To circumvent this, Hagan et al. introduced a square-root Kalman filter to estimate the system's covariance continuously, yielding the time-varying Liang-Kleeman (TvLK) causality formalism. Within this framework, the covariance matrix $P$ represents the state variance-covariance matrix (the Kalman estimation-error covariance), dynamically updated at the measurement and temporal update stages of the filter. The authors utilize the discrete-time linear stochastic state-space formulation, where the state vector $x_k$ and measurement vector $y_k$ are governed by process noise $w_k$ and measurement noise $v_k$, mapping to covariances $Q$ and $R$ respectively.A critical component of this methodology is the treatment of the process noise covariance $Q$ and observation noise covariance $R$. The authors acknowledge the inherent difficulty in determining these values, noting that previous attempts at TvLK implementations often erroneously assumed them to be constant. In the Hagan framework, $Q$ and $R$ are calculated offline prior to the active Kalman filter iteration, though the exact algorithmic calculation for these matrices is heavily expanded upon in later works.To prevent numerical instability—a well-documented issue in Kalman filtering when rounding errors accumulate in the covariance matrix computation—Hagan et al. applied the Bierman-Thornton UD covariance factorization algorithm. This square-root filtering algorithm uses modified Cholesky factors of the state variance-covariance matrix, ensuring that the matrix remains positive definite without requiring explicit square root operations, which stabilizes the calculation of the Kalman gain. In the TvLK methodology, LKIF is calculated at the analysis stage of the filter at every discrete time step, substituting the elements of the dynamically computed matrix $P$ for the traditional static sample covariance matrix $C$. The formulation was successfully validated on synthetic dynamical models and subsequently applied to real-world interactions between soil moisture and the atmosphere, revealing multiscale temporal interactions via wavelet transforms.2. Zhou et al. (2024) – Multivariate Time-Varying LK (MtvLK)Zhou et al. (2024) expanded the TvLK framework into multidimensional spaces, creating the Multivariate Time-Varying Liang-Kleeman Information Flow (MtvLK) algorithm. This extension is critical because geophysical systems are rarely bivariate; they are governed by webs of interacting variables where core drivers co-exist with numerous mediators and confounders.The MtvLK equation, stabilized by the Bierman-Thornton algorithm, is formalized as follows:$$T_{2\rightarrow1\vert{}3,4,...,d}^t(t) = \frac{1}{\det P} \sum_{j=1}^d \dot{\Delta}_{2j} P_{j,d1} \frac{P_{12}}{P_{11}}$$In this equation, $T$ represents the time-dependent information flow from variable $X_2$ to $X_1$, conditioned on variables $X_3$ through $X_d$. The matrix $P$ is the analysis estimation-error covariance matrix updated by the Kalman filter at time $t$, $\dot{\Delta}_{2j}$ represents the time-dependent cofactors of $P$, $P_{j,d1}$ is the cross-covariance between variable $X_j$ and the temporal derivative of $X_1$, and the ratio $P_{12}/P_{11}$ rescales the flow by the target variance.Zhou et al. (2024) significantly advanced the handling of process noise $Q(t)$ and observation noise $R(t)$. Rather than assuming them to be constant or calculating them statically offline, the MtvLK framework utilizes Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) and Unweighted Moving Average (UWMA) to estimate $Q$ and $R$ dynamically, feeding the results iteratively into the Kalman loop. However, the authors note a distinct methodological trade-off: utilizing EWMA and UWMA requires defining a lookback time window length, which results in the loss of an equivalent "information length" during the forward update of the filter. The authors present a mathematical solution to this missing window length information in the appendices of their manuscript, though the explicit algebraic equations for the EWMA/UWMA noise estimation remain omitted from the primary textual descriptions.The MtvLK method is highly computationally expensive compared to original static LKIF due to the continuous matrix inversions and factorizations required by the Kalman filter at every time step. The code for this framework is actively maintained and publicly archived at https://github.com/FeihongZhou/MtvLK.git.3. Siddique et al. (2026) – Conditioning in MtvLKBuilding directly upon the MtvLK architecture, Siddique et al. (2026) utilized the Kalman-filtered multivariate information flow to benchmark the roles of "conditioners" (mediators and confounders) in land-atmosphere interactions. This study explicitly relies on the evolving covariance $P(t)$ generated by the MtvLK filter to separate direct information flow from indirect contributions.Siddique et al. introduced four novel quantifiers to evaluate the contributions of these conditioners by examining the differential information flow ($\Delta IF$), which represents the discrepancy between bivariate and fully conditioned multivariate IF estimates. These metrics provide profound second-order insights into causal topologies:Mediator Dominance Index (MDI): Quantifies the proportion of a conditioner $m$ that contributes most to the total magnitude of information-flow restructuring. High MDI values identify variables that dominate the divergence between bivariate and multivariate couplings.Moderation Gain (MG): Measures the degree to which a conditioner enhances or suppresses the inferred coupling. High MG indicates that the conditioner is dampening the apparent driver influence that would otherwise be inflated in a simple bivariate signal.Confounding Pressure (CP): Measures how strongly the conditioner suppresses inflated bivariate influence, specifically isolating the confounding effect.Convergence Rate (CR): Quantifies the frequency at which the bivariate estimate aligns with the fully conditioned estimate, based on an absolute divergence threshold.The authors validated this framework by analyzing the influence of soil moisture (SM) and vapor pressure deficit (VPD) on Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) and Leaf Area Index (LAI). They discovered that under compound dry-hot conditions, VPD and temperature emerge as dominant mediators, causing massive divergence between bivariate and multivariate estimates. This research highlights that utilizing the MtvLK filter not only tracks causality over time but also explicitly identifies the physical conditions under which missing state variables corrupt causal inference. The scripts for computing these indices are available at https://github.com/FareehaSiddique/Conditioners-LKIF.4. Wang et al. (2025) – Causality-Driven Localization in EnKFWang et al. (2025) present a paradigm shift in the intersection of LKIF and filtering. Rather than using a Kalman filter to calculate LKIF, this framework uses LKIF to structurally optimize an Ensemble Adjustment Kalman Filter (EAKF) applied to Strongly Coupled Data Assimilation (SCDA).In standard ensemble-based DA, finite ensemble sizes generate severe sampling errors and spurious cross-component correlations. The traditional remedy is the Gaspari-Cohn (G-C) localization method, which empirically suppresses covariances based on isotropic (circular) geographical distance thresholds. However, geophysical interactions between distinct Earth system components (e.g., ocean-to-atmosphere versus atmosphere-to-ocean) are highly asymmetric and variable-dependent, rendering distance-based isotropic localization physically inappropriate.Wang et al. introduced a causality-driven localization method where LKIF quantitatively assesses the causal dependence strength between components across the spatial grid. In this architecture, the covariance $P$ represents the ensemble sample covariance ($P_f$, forecast covariance). LKIF generates an anisotropic, physically constrained localization matrix $L$. During the assimilation update, observations inside the optimal G-C radius may be discarded if LKIF determines they share no direct causal relationship with the target variable. Conversely, distant observations outside the G-C radius are included if LKIF proves direct causality.This method requires calculating LKIF before or during the assimilation cycle to generate the localization matrix, which is then applied via Schur product to the prior forecast covariance $P_f$. To combat variance collapse within the small ensemble sizes, Wang et al. simultaneously applied a constant multiplicative covariance inflation algorithm alongside the LKIF localization. The methodology was validated through twin experiments on an intermediate atmosphere-ocean-land coupled model, demonstrating that causality-driven localization significantly reduces Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and achieves faster stabilization than the G-C method. While the exact mathematical formulation mapping the LKIF causal strength to the localization matrix $L$ is omitted from the retrieved textual summary, the theoretical implication is clear: LKIF acts as a structural physical constraint on the Bayesian statistical update.C. Adjacent Papers and Filtering MechanismsWhile the direct intersection of LKIF and Kalman filtering is confined to the frameworks above, several adjacent methodologies provide necessary context regarding covariance modification and regularization.1. Graphical Lasso, Shrinkage, and Root Cause AnalysisIn industrial fault diagnosis, LKIF has been combined with the Graphical Lasso (Glasso) algorithm. Glasso operates as a high-precision dimensionality reduction method for large-scale datasets by applying an $L_1$ penalty (shrinkage/regularization) to the inverse covariance (precision) matrix. This forces the edge weights of spurious correlations to shrink exactly to zero, resulting in a sparse inverse covariance matrix. Once the sparse causal skeleton is learned via Glasso, LKIF is applied to calculate the precise magnitude and direction of the remaining information flows. While this does not use a Kalman filter, the mechanism of shrinking covariances prior to LKIF calculation is conceptually identical to the covariance localization applied in Wang et al. (2025).2. Fuzzy Cognitive Maps and Causal PriorsMachine learning models often treat inputs as flexible predictors optimized to minimize loss, remaining entirely agnostic to the underlying causal structure. Tyrovolas et al. addressed this by utilizing LKIF to identify actual causal relationships from data, subsequently imposing these directed flows as structural constraints (priors) in the learning procedure of Fuzzy Cognitive Maps (FCMs). This approach forces the machine learning model to respect the physical limits of information flow, ruling out spurious correlations and enhancing predictive power. The source code is maintained at marios-tyrovolas/Information-Flow-Based-Fuzzy-Cognitive-Maps-with-Enhanced-Interpretability. This mirrors the way Wang et al. (2025) uses LKIF as a prior constraint on the EAKF gain matrix.3. Non-Gaussian Ensemble Filtering and Adaptive InflationTo counteract filter divergence in EnKF, various covariance inflation techniques are employed. Constantinescu, Anderson, and Liang evaluated adaptive inflation techniques and non-Gaussian Rank Histogram Filters (RHF) for soil moisture DA. They demonstrated that adaptive multiplicative inflation—which increases the prior ensemble spread by a specific factor to offset systematic errors—is necessary for maintaining the optimal variance required for state estimation. Wang et al. (2025) explicitly paired LKIF localization with Anderson's multiplicative covariance inflation, proving that causality-preserving localization does not inherently replace the need to manually inflate the ensemble covariance to prevent collapse.D. Methodological Contradictions and Theoretical AmbiguitiesA profound analysis of the literature reveals a significant ontological contradiction between the theoretical foundations of LKIF and its application via sequential Kalman filters.The $C$ versus $P$ Substitution ContradictionThe original LKIF formulation, rigorously derived ab initio from Liouville equations and stochastic dynamical system theory, strictly requires the physical signal covariance (denoted as sample covariance $C$). This matrix $C$ is defined as $C_{ij} = \text{cov}(X_i, X_j)$, representing the actual physical variance and covariance of the state variables over a specified temporal window.However, in the TvLK and MtvLK frameworks introduced by Hagan (2019) and Zhou (2024), the authors extract the estimation-error covariance matrix $P(t)$ from the Kalman filter and substitute it directly into the LKIF formula in place of $C$. The matrix $P$ is formally defined as $P = \mathbb{E}[(x - \hat{x})(x - \hat{x})^T]$, representing the Bayesian uncertainty of the state estimate at a single point in time, heavily modulated by the observation noise $R$ and the process noise $Q$.This substitution relies on a fundamental heuristic leap. The uncertainty of a state estimate ($P$) does not inherently represent the physical variance of the signal ($C$) unless the state vector has been explicitly augmented to track temporal signal covariances. The literature justifies this substitution entirely through empirical validation on synthetic models, demonstrating that the output closely matches expected theoretical causality. However, the literature completely lacks a rigorous mathematical proof demonstrating that the evolution of Bayesian state uncertainty $P(t)$ is isomorphic to the physical information flow of the underlying dynamical system.The Impact of Low Observation Noise ($R$) on Causality RatiosIn the LKIF formula, the denominator contains the variance of the target variable ($P_{ii}$ or $C_{ii}$) to rescale the information flow. In optimal Kalman filtering, if the observation noise $R$ is exceptionally low (indicating highly accurate measurements), the posterior analysis covariance $P_a$ undergoes severe contraction, colloquially known as covariance collapse.If the variance $P_{ii}$ approaches zero due to this Bayesian contraction, dividing by a vanishing denominator can cause severe numerical instability and mathematical distortion in the LKIF ratio. While Hagan (2019) and Zhou (2024) mitigate computational round-off errors using the Bierman-Thornton UD factorization algorithm, which stabilizes the modified Cholesky factors during matrix updates, the literature does not explicitly address the physical distortion of the causal ratio itself when $R \to 0$. The assumption remains that the UD factorization prevents the denominator from generating infinite causal values, but the theoretical distinction between expected Kalman posterior contraction and finite-ensemble covariance collapse within the context of LKIF remains unaddressed.E.
