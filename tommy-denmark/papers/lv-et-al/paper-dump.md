Causal Links Among Sources of Heat in the Arctic Surface
Mixed Layer and Seasonal Sea Ice Melt
Xinyuan Lv1 , Yangjun Wang2, Huizan Wang1 , Kaijun Ren1 , and Yu Cao1
1College of Meteorology and Oceanology, National University of Defense Technology, Changsha, China, 2College of
Advanced Interdisciplinary Studies, National University of Defense Technology, Nanjing, China
Abstract In recent decades, the ocean's influence on Arctic sea ice has become increasingly pronounced.
The surface mixed layer (SML) serves as a critical interface between the underlying ocean and sea ice,
mediating heat transfer between them. However, the links between the SML and seasonal sea ice changes, as
well as the dominant mechanisms governing this interaction, remain insufficiently understood. This study uses
in situ sea‐ice drift data and the Liang–Kleeman information flow to quantify the causal relationship between the
SML and sea ice thickness on the Eurasian Basin side, revealing a clear two‐way cause‐effect relationship.
Furthermore, the links between seven heat sources in the SML and seasonal sea‐ice melt are also quantified.
Vertical turbulence emerges as the primary contributor to heat transfer in the SML near Fram Strait and north of
Svalbard, where the halocline is weak and Atlantic Water (AW) remains relatively warm. When the SML heat
content is high, horizontal advection dominates, while vertical advection becomes more important only when
AW is ventilated upward. In contrast, in the Nansen and Amundsen basins, where the influence of AW heat is
weaker, the relationship between the SML and seasonal sea‐ice melt is primarily driven by sea‐ice melting, with
the SML itself being influenced mainly by vertical turbulence and ice‐ocean heat forcing.
Plain Language Summary Heat flows between the ocean's top layer and sea ice in the Arctic,
influencing seasonal sea ice changes. Yet, it is unclear how this layer drives sea ice changes seasonally, or which
key processes make this connection work. Using measurements taken directly from drifting ice stations, we
discover a complex two‐way relationship: near warmer Atlantic currents (e.g., Svalbard), heat rising from
deeper ocean layers is the main driver of sea ice melt. In the central Arctic basins farther east, where these warm
currents are weaker, the situation flips: the melting sea ice itself becomes the main force changing ocean heat
near the surface. By showing where and how ocean heat drives sea ice melt versus where the melting sea ice
itself dominates, we confirm that warm Atlantic currents entering the Arctic are a major control for sea ice melt,
but only in specific regions. Current climate models often misrepresent these key Atlantic currents. Our real‐
world measurements provide concrete targets for improving model accuracy, especially in simulating how heat
rises through ocean layers. Understanding these different interactions is crucial for improving predictions of
how quickly Arctic sea ice will disappear as the climate continues to warm.
1. Introduction
The Arctic has been undergoing drastic climate change in recent decades (Landrum & Holland, 2020) owing to
rapid changes in the sea ice, atmosphere, and ocean (Kikuchi et al., 2021). The reduction in sea ice varies
considerably across the Arctic (Close et al., 2015; Onarheim et al., 2018), particularly along the path of AW
(Ivanov et al., 2012; Polyakov et al., 2017, Polyakov, Alkire, et al., 2020, Polyakov, Rippeth, et al., 2020;
Smedsrud et al., 2013). The Eurasian Basin has undergone significant sea ice loss in recent years (Herbaut
et al., 2022; Polyakov et al., 2017), particularly in the Fram Strait (Hansen et al., 2013; Spreen et al., 2020; Wang
et al., 2023), the Northern Svalbard (NS) (Ivanov et al., 2018; Onarheim et al., 2014), and the Barents Sea (Årthun
et al., 2012; Lind et al., 2018). This rapid decline in Arctic sea ice amplifies global warming and influences
atmospheric and oceanic circulations (Shu et al., 2022). A comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms
driving the seasonal variability of sea ice is crucial for projecting future changes and evaluating their societal
impacts.

(SML; Figure 1) serves as a medium for the storage, distribution, and release of seasonal heat (Carmack

The relative contributions of various heat sources of the SML, particularly from the subsurface ocean, to vari-
ations in seasonal sea ice have not been systematically explored. Research on the SML has predominantly relied

budget equation have overlooked certain terms (Polyakov et al., 2013; Zhong et al., 2022). This study utilizes 14
sets of in situ sea‐ice drift data collected during different expeditions in the distinctly “Atlantified” Fram Strait,

understanding of the ocean's impact on Arctic sea ice.
To explore whether seasonal sea ice melt primarily influences the SML or the reverse, we use Liang–Kleeman
information flow (Liang, 2021) in conjunction with traditional correlation analysis to examine the interactions
between the SML (including its different heat sources) and seasonal changes in sea ice. Unlike correlation
analysis, methods based on information flow consider temporal derivatives to provide directionality and establish
credible causal relationships (Docquier et al., 2022; Dörr et al., 2024). A deeper understanding of the causal
relationships between the SML, its heat sources, and seasonal sea ice variability has the potential to offer new
insights and perspectives for improving climate models, thereby enabling more precise projections of future sea
ice changes.

2. Data, Design and Methods
2.1. Data
Most of the in situ sea‐ice drift data (lagrangian measurements of seawater profiles) were obtained from the
Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI) and include information on temperature and salinity collected by
Ice‐Tethered Profilers (ITPs). The geographical and temporal ranges of the data are provided in Table 1. All ITP
in situ sea‐ice drift data have a temporal resolution of approximately 6 hr “N‐ICE2015” refers to the Norwegian
Young Sea ICE Expedition and “MOSAiC” refers to the Multidisciplinary drifting Observatory for the Study of
Arctic Climate. Both campaigns use the Microstructure Sonde System (MSS) for the temperature and salinity, and
Acoustic Doppler Current Profilers (ADCPs) for the horizontal current (Baumann et al., 2021; Fer et al., 2016;
Meyer et al., 2016; Schulz et al., 2023). To align these heterogeneous data sets with the ITP data, a common
temporal resolution of approximately 6 hr is established. This is achieved through the following processing: (a)
The high‐frequency ADCP time series (original sampling: ∼1 hr for N‐ICE2015, ∼20 min for MOSAiC) are
subsampled at 6– to 8–hr intervals. (b) The MSS profiles, which feature intensive sampling bursts separated by
gaps of several hours to a day, are temporally matched to all subsampled ADCP data points. This conservative
approach treats each MSS measurement as representative of the turbulent conditions during that period, thereby
avoiding the introduction of spurious signals through interpolation. Overall, the processed in situ sea‐ice drift data

physical processes we aim to capture. Consequently, removing this trend would eliminate the key spatial gradient
effect, which is the focus of our study, and would distort the true causal relationship between changes in sea ice
state and changes in environmental factors (such as the temperature of SML). Our research primarily emphasizes
the analysis of local, short‐term interactions occurring along the spatial path of the drift trajectory.
2.2. Design
To clarify the contribution of oceanic thermal processes (such as the temperature of SML) to seasonal sea ice
variations and their driving mechanisms, this study employs a two‐step causal analysis approach:
First (Section 3.1), we examine the causal relationship between the mixed‐layer‐averaged sea surface temperature
(TSML) and SIT/concentration (SIT/SIC). This step aims to confirm whether the ocean's thermal state (TSML)
constitutes an effective physical driver influencing the seasonal evolution of sea ice.

atmospheric thermal and dynamical influences) and quantitatively evaluate its internal driving processes, we
introduce a theoretical variable: Potential Ice Thickness Melt (SITpot):

intervals (<6 hr) between consecutive points in the in situ sea‐ice drift data used in this study, coupled with the

3. Results
We primarily investigate the Fram Strait, the NS, and the Eurasian Basin. The Nansen Basin and the Amundsen
Basin within the Eurasian Basin are divided by Gakkel Ridge, with 90°E serving as the boundary between their
eastern and western sections. The division of the study area and trajectories of drift are shown in Figure 2. Owing
to the small size of the Fram Strait, strictly defining its boundaries using latitude and longitude would have
resulted in a limited number of data profiles to influence the reliability of the results. Therefore, we extend its
range and incorporate a small portion of data from the NS for the Fram Strait analysis, defining this area as the
Fram Strait Extended Area (FSEA). To maintain data provenance, the FSEA and the NS are initially treated as
separate geographical entities during data collection and documentation. However, given their similar oceanic

3.2.2. Nansen Basin
Moving northeast from the NS into the Nansen Basin, the oceanic environment undergoes a pronounced tran-

compared to the FSEA and the NS. This section explores how these altered oceanic conditions modulate the
causal relationships between mixed‐layer heat sources and SITpot.

increased IOHF. The increased heat in the SML both enhanced SITpot (τIOHF→SITpot = 8.2%) and induced turbulence.

absorption from the ocean reduced outward heat dissipation, further increasing IOHF (τSITpot→IOHF = 10.03%).

3.2.3. Amundsen Basin
Mid‐depth warm AW flows in the Arctic basins to gradually dissipate their
heat. By the time it reaches the Amundsen Basin, its temperature decreases,
its upper depth increases, and its heat transfer to the upper layers weakens
(Figures 6, 7b–7d, 8b, and 8c). Furthermore, the internal ocean in the Amundsen Basin is stable, with a stronger
stratification between the SML and AWL. Nevertheless, oceanic heat sources still exhibit certain causal re-
lationships with SITpot. The in situ sea‐ice drifts of ITP57, and ITP129 in the WAB, as well as ITP57 in the EAB
reveal that SITpot exerts the strongest influence on IOHF (Figure 8a).
Additionally, both western and eastern ITP57 drifts identify vertical turbulence as the primary oceanic heat
contributor to SITpot. In these drifts, significantly weakened stratification (Figures 8b and 8c) enhances vertical
turbulence, and drives SITpot increase. This SITpot change disrupts the SML's thermohaline structure, triggering
MLD deepening and heat redistribution. These adjustments: (a) Further modify vertical turbulence. (b) Increase
oceanic heat dissipation and then reduce IOHF.
Compared to the Nansen Basin, the more stable and quiescent of the water column in the Amundsen Basin results
in a more constrained oceanic influence: SITpot primarily affects vertical turbulence and IOHF, while its
discernible impact on MLD‐related heat sources—as observed in the Nansen Basin—is no longer present.
4. Discussion
This study reveals a noteworthy phenomenon: in approximately 25% of the profiles (Figure 3), SIT and the mixed‐
layer‐averaged temperature (TSML) exhibit a positive correlation, with some profiles (e.g., ITP94) even showing a
statistically significant two‐way causal relationship. This differs from the commonly expected pattern where
oceanic thermal melting leads to a negative correlation between SIT and TSML.

Physically, this positive correlation is primarily attributed to the insulating effect of sea ice. A thicker sea ice layer
effectively suppresses heat loss from the relatively warmer underlying seawater to the cold atmosphere (Rigor
et al., 2002). This allows the SML beneath thicker ice to maintain a relatively higher temperature (or experience a
slower cooling rate during the cold season). Crucially, despite the higher underlying seawater temperature, the
physical barrier of the sea ice itself limits the direct and efficient upward conduction of this heat to the ice‐ocean
interface to drive melting. Consequently, over specific observational timescales, an increase in SIT (e.g., driven
by atmospheric processes) may coincide with a relative increase (or delayed cooling) in the underlying TSML,
manifesting as a positive correlation SIT and TSML.
More importantly, causal analysis indicates that in these positive correlation cases, the dominant causal direction
is almost universally SIT → TSML. This strongly suggests that changes in SIT are the leading factor driving
changes in TSML, rather than vice versa. To trace the origin of SIT changes, we further examine the causal re-
lationships between atmospheric forcing (thermal: surface energy budget SEB; dynamic: sea ice velocity SIV)
and SIT (Figure 9). Analysis of the causal drivers reveals that for the majority of these cases (4 out of 5, expect for
EAB‐ITP91), SIT is significantly forced by at least one atmospheric factor: either SEB or SIV exhibits a causal
influence on SIT (SEB/SIV → SIT) alongside a negative correlation with it. This supports a proposed causal chain
that is valid for the majority of our study cases: atmospheric thermal or dynamical forcing (SEB/SIV) first drives
changes in SIT. This change in SIT (e.g., thickening) then influences the underlying TSML (e.g., by maintaining or
slowing cooling via its insulating effect), which ultimately manifests as a positive correlation between SIT
and TSML.
Therefore, these observed positive correlations do not negate the role of oceanic thermal processes in overall sea
ice melt. Instead, they reflect a complex process occurring under specific conditions and spatiotemporal scales
(such as the daily changes focused on in this study): atmospheric forcing modulates the feedback of sea ice on the
underlying oceanic thermal state (insulating effect) by altering the sea ice state (thickness). This highlights the
complexity of atmosphere‐sea ice‐ocean system interactions during the seasonal evolution of Arctic sea ice.
In the in situ sea‐ice drift data from ITP58 in the WAB and ITP129 in the EAB, the results in Section 3.1 explicitly
identify a clear causal relationship in the TSML → SIT direction. Combined with the overall trend in SIT and TSML
in these two data sets (figure omitted) and the negative correlation between them, a decrease in TSML leads to an
increase in SIT. However, influenced by other factors such as the freezing point temperature, the evolution of
SITpot (calculated from the mixed‐layer heat content Q, see details in Section 2.2) does not show a monotonic
increase like SIT. Furthermore, no significant causal relationship exists between individual heat sources in the
SML and SITpot. This strongly suggests that the statistical significance of TSML → SIT may reflect covariation
between oceanic thermal processes and sea ice changes, but other concurrent physical mechanisms (e.g., strong
atmospheric cooling, sea ice dynamics) are likely the dominant forces driving SIT increase. In the Amundsen
Basin, the actual contribution of the oceanic thermal mechanism to SIT changes is limited.
5. Conclusions and Future Work
We used in situ sea‐ice drift data sets from WHOI, and N‐ICE2015 and MOSAiC expeditions in conjunction with
the equation for the heat budget of the surface mixed layer to investigate the causal relationships between SIT/sea
ice concentration (SIC) and the average temperature of the mixed layer (TSML) at daily time scale on the Eurasian
Basin side—specifically in the FSEA, the NS, the Nansen Basin, and the Amundsen Basin. The use of in situ sea‐
ice drift data sets allowed us to quantify the terms in Equation 1 on actual seawater flow trajectories, an aspect
often overlooked in previous studies (Herbaut et al., 2022; Mukherjee & Ravichandran, 2023). One of the primary
innovations of this study is the application of the Liang–Kleeman method of information flow, which surpasses
conventional correlation analysis by distinguishing correlation from causation and identifying the direction of
potential causal relationships (Dörr et al., 2024). Based on this method, the dominant influencing factors between
the SML and seasonal sea ice melt were investigated. To isolate the contribution of the oceanic thermal mech-
anism, we introduced the potential maximum melt thickness (SITpot) metric—an upper bound estimate of total
SIT reduction attributable to the average temperature of the surface mixed layer (TSML)—and further investigated
its causal links with seven heat sources within the SML: HADV, vertical turbulence, TMLD, lateral advection,
VADV, IOHF, and sea‐ice melting/formation.

We identify significant two‐way causal relationships between SIT and TSML in all regions. There is nearly no

The temperature of AW exhibits a considerably high intensity (characterized by elevated temperatures and

SITpot becomes the dominant factor driving changes in heat sources within the SML rather than the reverse in the
Nansen Basin and the Amundsen Basin. Unlike the FSEA and the NS, where AW layer uplift drives heat source
variations within the SML, the causal relationships between mixed‐layer heat sources and SITpot in these two
basins are primarily governed by mixed‐layer depth variability. Vertical turbulence and IOHF emerge as the
dominant heat sources influenced by SITpot, with the latter generally contributing slightly more than the former.
This highlights the critical role of oceanic heat dissipation toward the sea ice base or the atmosphere in the
Eurasian Basin.
This study reveals that approximately 25% of profiles exhibit a positive correlation between TSML and SIT,
contrasting the expected negative correlation from oceanic melting. Mechanistically, this arises primarily from
the insulating effect of thicker sea ice, which reduces upward heat flux to the atmosphere, allowing warmer
underlying water but limiting conductive heat transfer to the sea ice base for melting. Crucially, causal analysis
identifies SIT → TSML as the dominant pathway in these cases, driven by atmospheric forcing (surface energy
budget/sea ice velocity) altering SIT. These positive correlations highlight the complex interplay where atmo-
spheric forcing modulates the ocean's thermal state via SIT changes, underscoring the nuanced atmosphere‐sea
ice‐ocean feedbacks operating at daily scales during Arctic seasonal evolution.
The analysis in this study is based on a limited number of in situ sea‐ice drift data sets (lagrangian trajectories).
These drifts exhibit substantial seasonal variability even within the same region. While this approach provides
unique insights into ice‐ocean interactions along specific pathways, it may not fully capture the regional
climatology. The causal mechanisms identified are likely subject to seasonal modulation. For instance, the cause‐
effect relationship may differ between the ice growth season and the melt season. Consequently, our findings,
derived from particular trajectories and time periods, represent a snapshot of these complex interactions and their
stability across seasons warrants further investigation. Consequently, our findings highlight the need for and point
toward future targeted data collection across diverse regions and seasons to better constrain the broader appli-
cability and seasonal dependence of these causal mechanisms.
Given the inherent uncertainties associated with reanalysis‐based SIT data, as discussed in the Data section, a
logical future step would be to verify and extend the conclusions of this study using contemporaneous in situ
thickness measurements, should they become available. Such efforts will be crucial to disentangle the actual
physical signals from the inherent biases and uncertainties of the reanalysis product.

basal melt would, meaning actual melt attributable to SML would be less than or equal to SITpot. Our study
intentionally focuses on establishing the causal links between SITpot and different heat sources in the SML,
isolating the contribution of the heat sources themselves. The modulation of this potential by environmental
factors like SIT and SIC is a crucial next step but lies outside the primary driver‐focused scope of this analysis. We

Beyond its role as a heat sink in winter, leads also play a critical role in summer by allowing enhanced absorption
of solar short‐wave radiation, which is not explicitly accounted for in our surface‐mixed‐layer heat budget.
Neglecting this process may introduce uncertainty in our analysis, particularly in regions or seasons with high
lead fraction.
Starting from the SML, this study confirms that the ocean's influence on sea ice is closely tied to the mid‐layer
AW in the Arctic. However, current simulations often exhibit biases, with the AW layer being too thick and too
deep (Heuzé et al., 2023; Holloway et al., 2007; Ilıcak et al., 2016; Khosravi et al., 2022; Shu et al., 2019). Our
findings underscore the necessity of improving the representation of Arctic mid‐layer AW in numerical models.
From a physical perspective, the study identifies critical aspects requiring attention for model improvement, such
as the further refinement of vertical turbulent diffusion parameterization schemes.
In future research, we plan to use model data with relatively better simulation performance to extend this study to
longer time scales and broader regions. This would allow us to validate and refine the mechanisms identified in
this work, with the goal of deriving more generalizable conclusions.
